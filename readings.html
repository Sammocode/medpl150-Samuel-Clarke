Reflection #1

Photography and Image Making

When reading this chapter of Susan Sontag’s “In Plato’s Cave,” photography is not passive documentation, but rather a powerful act of framing reality, an editorial gesture that shapes how we see, remember, and interpret the world. For me, image making is not just about capturing what’s in front of the camera lens; it’s about isolating emotional truth from visual clutter (the focus on that subject away from the background being blurry). Whether I’m editing a photo for a website or writing my opinions about the media shaping people, I’m always asking myself: What does this image say when stripped of context? And what context do I choose to restore?

Ever since I remember reading “In Plato's Cave” for an assignment in my freshman year in community college, my English professor was a well-known photographer like "Her Hair Reveals" at the Rio Gallery Visual Conversation with Afro Brazilian Women" and putting them through a museum just to interact an intimate chat and find the meanings within the language of photography. Photography, when practiced, is a form of capturing absurd rarity or curiosity. I've edited photos on my phone, from family moments to special events, maintaining subtle clarity in the background to reframe, not erase, their history. I’ve built character designs, sketches, and creative writing that use visual contrast to bring to mind symbolic meaning. Each image is like a gesture of looking deeper, a boundary-setting act that says: This is what I choose to remember. This is how I choose to see.

Sontag had warned us of the camera’s power to objectify, to turn people into subjects and moments into trophies. The camera was sold like a predatory weapon, one that is as automatic a capture as possible, ready to spring at the trigger like a gun. I resist that by treating each project as part of a larger emotional personal arc. Across these ten assignments this semester, I won’t just be switching tools; I’ll be consistent in building a portfolio of work that reflects my evolving relationship with visual technical media. From photography to illustration, from Photoshop to image-text hybrids within a website using HTML/CSS, I’ll be exploring how visuals can carry emotional weight, symbolic depth, and narrative clarity.

In representing our modern world, I’m drawn to contrast: the tension between digital saturation and emotional authenticity. A single photo of a quiet background street of Brooklyn can say more than a thousand filtered selfies of a human being. A graphic that contrasts archival footage with contemporary still images has the potential to bring up generational tensions, similar to those observed within animation and gaming franchises I have researched and analyzed. By utilizing both imagery and text, my objective is to examine concepts of belonging and transition, as well as satirically illustrate the journey from the "weave tumbles" of disorder to the "garden" of coherence. Today’s cameras aren’t toys, they’re fixtures in our environment, shaping how we confirm and consume reality., touchstones and confirmation to prove of reductive approach to reality which is considered realistic. Photographs are seen as information to users who aren't read-savvy, but instead for visual learners; photography is a camera record.

This personal ethic informs not just how I see, but how I build, across platforms, across assignments. Each project will be a scene in that arc. The first may be raw, the second more refined, and by the tenth, I hope to arrive at a visual language that feels fully integrated. I’ll use photography not just to capture, but to curate. I’ll use illustration and Photoshop not just to decorate, but to symbolize. And I’ll use image-text combinations to speak in layered tones, where what’s seen and what’s said reinforce each other. The limit of photographic knowledge of the world is that, while it can gain consciousness, it can never be ethical or political knowledge. Today, everything exists to end in a photograph.

This isn’t just my required coursework but rather my creative arc journey within the portfolio I'm producing. And like Sontag’s Plato’s cave, I’m stepping out of the cave, not just into light, but into the lens. Not just to see, but to frame the world with satirical criticism. My portfolio isn’t a record, it’s a reckoning.



Reflection #2

Slopstream: A Non-Creative Subject with No Originality

In Mailk’s article on Generative AI is critically sharp and urgent about how it is reshaping the visual medium of psychological fabrication of our reality. Her term of “AI Slop” definitely shows the flood of low effort, low quality, already database-generated content by its algorithmic that tries to mimic the media artifacts of images and videos of human historical works, but it doesn’t have the authenticity of authorship, intent, and the lack of accountability. What makes this writing piece resonate with me is that it's not just critiquing or kissing ass to technology, but formulating a deeper cultural drift, a collective sleepwalk into a world where truth is no longer circulated in facts.

Malik is straight to the point, issuing a warning to us about misinformation more in depth; she shows that the erosion of visual credibility is already underway. Generative AI Content isn’t just abstract nonsense; it's an extension of traditional propaganda, it's designed to provoke, distort, and circulate without involving any real people or the physical constraints of real life, of delusional and nonsensical scenarios. Her analysis decoding on WhatsApp is disturbing, vile about a relative receiving fake war footage, it's not rare anymore, it's become normalized, of this disgusting bullshit. These platforms don’t care about showing genuine content or fake news on their site, just about profit and ads. Even though it's revolting, the content has no neutrality to it, just biases up to eleven of the slop because all bad publicity is good publicity, driving engagement.

What stands out is Mailk’s insistence that it’s a design feature by default and not some type of glitch, that produces and distributes AI slop built around the system of clout and clicks over truth and justice, spectacle over substance. Of course, it's shared through private channels on WhatsApp to bypass scrutiny, causing intimate distortion of public misinformation, delivered by the services of people that we trust. Her tone isn’t alarming; it's reflective, almost worrying. Besides critiquing technology, it’s the grieving of the loss of shared visual language of human to human, instead it's bot to human of humans using bots around with no effort whatsoever. Everything is synthetic imagery nowadays; the line between real and fake is melted together, containing consequences. It becomes skeptical when people no longer trust what they see in any medium and try to seek better and raw knowledge, where the society of the spectacle of a mass delusion.

In this reflection, what matters to me the most is that it reinforces the importance of integrity authorship of intention. In my own works, that is, anything such as visual, written, or symbolic language, I will resist the flattening effect of AI slop. Her article reminds me that the meaning of human-made work must be curated, not generative. These images must carry psychological aftermath, not just style. There must be some hope of truth in the world of distortion; it must be grounded and believable. The closing warning isn’t just tech but about cultural fatigue, that where sleepwalking into a disaster, of sensory overload. Malik is just telling what we need to hear to wake us up against AI, not to fear it, reappropriating of protecting what was really our authenticity of the images that we create and consume for each other, of human-to-human content, no bots allowed.

For your information, AI generated doesn't actually "create" content, it recombines by pulling from known databases, often copyrighted infringement (Not in the fair use, type of sense) saturated material, and stitches patterns based on probability, not intent. Humans on the other hand create from context, growth, and psychological geometry. We mark transitions, invent new forms, and build legacies that aren't just secondary, they're origin stories, AI can't imitate that out of nowhere!


Reflection #3

Illustrating Grief:

The Visual of Maternal Loss!

The illustration in Kadiatu’s story acts as a protective shield, warning viewers from the raw trauma while experiencing its weight and stress. Unlike photography, which captures literal and real moments, illustration allows for abstraction, metaphor, and expressive representation that enhance the viewer’s understanding of grief and loss.

In Chapter 3, Kadiatu’s story titled “The devil at my door” uses illustrations to explore the unendurable realm of maternal grief. After losing her children due to delayed medical care, Kadiatu’s pain is personal but also symbolizes systemic failure. The illustrations are raw, textured, and passionate. They do not seek realism but instead evoke a sense of haunting isolation. For instance, the image of Kadiatu standing alone in a dark doorway, with shadowy figures behind her, conveys both literal danger and metaphorical distress. This visual abstraction allows viewers to connect with the depth of their experience without the voyeurism that photography might wrongly suggest.

Compared to the photographs used, which document real environments, and daily life, the illustrations in Kadiatu’s chapter offer levelheadedness rather than factual detail. Photographs can show what happened, but illustrations can show how it felt. This is especially important in stories of trauma, where authenticity may be more urgent than visual accuracy. The stylized sketches also allow for iconography: the “devil” at her door is a representation of fear, loss, and systemic neglect. This graphic novel also offers privacy and self-respect. In a context where photography might expose subjects, drawing allows the storyteller to retain control over how their image is shared. Kadiatu’s illustrated likeness is a symbolic avatar, shaped by her story rather than just standard images. This protects her identity while still bringing a feeling of her experience.

In comparison to just text alone, illustration bridges the gap between language and thought. While Kadiatu’s words describe her grief, the visuals express narrative, making it accessible to viewers who may not share her cultural or semantic background. The combination of text and illustration creates a multitude of empathy that viewers read her story, relate to her pain, and feel the weight through visual metaphor. Ultimately, the use of illustration in Kadiatu’s chapter is not just aesthetic; it’s ethical. It allows her story to be told with perceptiveness, significance, and protective care. It transforms her grief into a visual language that demands recognition without exploitation. In a series that documents maternal crisis, illustration becomes a tool of resistance, refusing to let trauma be reduced into data or spectacle. It insists on empathy, lucidity, honor and grace; that's was always matters at the end of the day.
