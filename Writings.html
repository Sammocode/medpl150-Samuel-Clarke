Artist Remarks

I am a media developer drawn to the emotional and narrative possibilities of interactive worlds. Whether I’m working in film, animation, or game design, I’m motivated by the challenge of expressing ideas that are difficult to communicate through traditional forms. My background moves across disciplines, from character design in high school to programming, web development, and Information Technology, and that blend shapes how I build digital experiences today. I’m mostly at home where storytelling meets systems, where mechanics and visual language work together to create meaning.

When exploring media that lets characters, choices, and environments speak for themselves. Video games, animation, and visual storytelling influence me not just as entertainment but as frameworks for understanding memory, identity, and transformation. These ideas guide my current work developing original IPs and building my personal brand, Westward Equinox Studios, as a space for experimentation and authorship. I’m especially interested in how people connect with fictional identities and how interactivity can evoke emotion through movement, decision-making, and world-building.

People often ask how I balance the technical and creative sides of my work, and the answer is that I don’t separate them; I continue to build them even after college. My projects aim for transparency, resonance, and intention, work that feels authored rather than manufactured. I’m building toward a future in game development and media production where I can expand both my creative control and technical flow. This marks the direction of my journey and the stories that I’m continuing to be committed to telling.



Written Course #2

What I Hear, A Soundwalk

This Wednesday afternoon, walking through my old childhood neighborhood in Brooklyn, between Glenwood Rd and Flatbush Junction area, it sounds exactly like a familiar soundscape feels. The construction noises, distant drills, and growing up the constant reshaping of buildings echo the rhythms. Birds chirping above the trees, while motorbikes rev their engines along the rough grit of the sidewalk beneath my shoes and feet. Every so often, church bells ring out in a patterned motif that marks the passing of time.

Between 3:13 and 4:30 p.m., the neighborhood shifts. A man sweeps dust from the front of his house, while planes roar dimly above the skies. As traffic thickens, drivers shout and honk to keep moving on the streets. MTA and School Buses exhale their loud, mechanical sighs by idling and rumbling near my old school, as their engines form a steady street backdrop. At the park by Nostrand Playground, children screaming and laughing, basketballs bounce sharply against the court, and older boys hype each other up after every shot. Squirrels munching in the early spring trees, while the honking and beeping of Albany streets' traffic never fades.

Passing through P.S. 198 and I.S. 109 brings memories of after-school programs with clapping rhythms. As I continue walking, I hear the ice cream truck’s melody, water hoses spraying cars at repair shops, gas station pumps buzzing, and even a small metallic tinkering sound from a nearby house. My neighborhood is urban, being layered; while time has changed, but also stayed the same.


Written Course #3

Editing Analysis: PlayTime (1967) – The Waiting Room

Scene: Jacques Tati - Playtime - The Waiting Room

In this sequence, Tati uses detached, rhythmic editing to shown the absurdity of modern international style. The scene is defined by continuity editing that feels intentionally rigid. Tati relies heavily on long takes and wide shots of angles, so when a cut does occur, it usually serves a specific spatial purpose not dramatic. This scene maintains a strict adherence to the 180-degree rule, give a senses of the flat, grid-like geometry of the office building. There are several eyeline matches between Mr. Hulot and the Man sitting on the other side of the room in a glass-encased boxed, but Tati subverts the traditional POV shot. Instead of seeing exactly what Hulot sees, we often remain in a wide "objective" shot, making Hulot appear like a small man in a massive scale.

The most striking strategy is the rhythmic cutting based on sound. A cut often occurs precisely when a footstep echoes, creating a "mechanical" pace. The rectangular shapes of the black cushy chairs in rectangular glass partitions of the next shot gives a repetitive, stern environment. Movement within the shots is often synchronized; as one character exits frame-left, the next shot picks up the motion from the right, maintaining a seamless but hauntingly robotic flow. These choices serve Tati’s intention to critique the coldness of modern architecture. By avoiding close-ups and using wide-angle compositional editing, he forces the viewer to find the "joke" within the frame, reflecting the disorientation around Mr. Hulot.
